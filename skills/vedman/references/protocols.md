# VEDMAN Protocols

## High-stakes decision

1. State the decision and deadline.
2. Define the real target and non-negotiables.
3. Build an evidence ledger: facts, assumptions, unknowns, emotions.
4. Identify the status-quo option; it is still a choice.
5. Compare no more than three viable options.
6. Score consequences: upside, downside, probability, reversibility, time, cash, relationships, reputation, and attention.
7. Identify the strongest disconfirming evidence for the leading option.
8. Decide or name the one missing fact that prevents a rational decision.
9. Set the first action, owner, deadline, success measure, and review trigger.

Use rough numbers when precision is unavailable. Do not disguise guesses as calculations.

## CEO and commercial decision

Ask only the questions capable of changing the conclusion:

- What cash enters, when, and with what collection risk?
- What is the true delivery cost and gross margin?
- Which bottleneck limits growth: leads, conversion, pricing, capacity, quality, or collection?
- What recurring management load does this create?
- Does this improve leverage or purchase another demanding job?
- What must stop if this begins?
- What is the walk-away condition?

Recommend a position, not merely a list of possibilities.

## Negotiation

1. Define the target, floor, walk-away point, and best alternative.
2. Separate economic interests from ego and symbolism.
3. Map the other side's incentives, constraints, alternatives, and likely fears.
4. Seek structures that align incentives before arguing over percentages.
5. Convert verbal enthusiasm into written scope, money, ownership, authority, dates, and exit terms.
6. Never bluff about facts that can be verified.
7. Preserve the relationship only when doing so does not require abandoning the floor.

## Crisis and emotional activation

1. Stop irreversible action while anger, fear, humiliation, euphoria, or desperation is dominant.
2. Regulate the body: slow breathing, water, food, movement, sleep, or physical distance as appropriate.
3. Separate actual damage from imagined future damage.
4. Identify what must be protected in the next hour, day, and week.
5. Select the smallest stabilizing action.
6. Communicate facts without accusation or dramatic prediction.
7. Reassess after the nervous system settles or material evidence changes.

Do not use delay when immediate safety action is required.

## Performance rehearsal

1. Choose one specific upcoming situation.
2. Rehearse the environment, opening behavior, difficult moment, recovery, and close.
3. Imagine controllable behavior rather than applause, money, or victory alone.
4. Practise physically or verbally when possible.
5. Enter the real situation with one process cue.
6. Review execution without converting mistakes into identity judgments.

## Habit installation

1. Define: “I am the kind of person who [observable behavior].”
2. Attach the behavior to a stable cue.
3. Make the first version small enough to repeat under stress.
4. Remove one friction point from the environment.
5. Track completion, not mood.
6. After a miss, resume at the next cue; do not create a failure identity.

## Daily command

- Must-win result: one outcome that makes the day materially successful.
- Supporting outcomes: no more than three.
- First decisive action: concrete and schedulable.
- Likely DRIFT signal: one risk to watch.
- Stop-doing item: one low-value activity to refuse or defer.
- Shutdown: record result, correction, and tomorrow's first move.

## Weekly recalibration

1. Record wins as evidence, not self-congratulation alone.
2. Record misses as system feedback, not shame.
3. Review the life domains relevant to the current season.
4. Identify the single biggest bottleneck.
5. Decide what to continue, correct, stop, delegate, and begin.
6. Set one 7-day target and its scoreboard.
7. Check whether the 7-day target still serves the 90-day direction.

## Relationship and boundary decision

1. Describe behavior without mind-reading.
2. State the impact and the need or boundary.
3. Distinguish a one-time mistake from a repeated pattern.
4. Consider the other person's agency and constraints.
5. Make a clear request with a consequence the user can actually enforce.
6. Avoid punishment disguised as a boundary.
7. Judge change by sustained behavior, not promises made under pressure.
